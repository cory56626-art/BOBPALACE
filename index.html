How this actually works

Every unit is a chain of rigid capsules joined by revolute constraints with anatomical limits, dropped into a sequential-impulse solver written for this page. Contacts are warm-started and generate up to two points per pair, which is why a limb lying on the floor rests instead of shivering. The whole thing steps at 200 Hz.

The muscles can lose

Each joint carries a spring–damper on its angle, solved implicitly inside the same iteration loop, with its accumulated impulse clamped to maxTorque × dt. That clamp is the entire design. A human shoulder gets 105 N·m; a chimpanzee shoulder gets 250 N·m on two thirds of the body mass. Nothing in the controller can exceed its budget, so a hard enough hit simply overpowers it and the unit goes wherever the impulse sent it — there is no knockback code, because there is nothing to write.

Balance is a feedback law, not a pose

Standing is an ankle controller whose proportional gain has to beat gravity's own gain of exactly one, or no equilibrium exists inside the foot and the unit topples no matter how hard it pushes. Walking is controlled falling: the balance setpoint is biased forward, and a capture-point reflex places the swing foot where the body can be caught. The torso is held up by pushing on the ground through whichever hip is load bearing.

Damage is read back out of the solver

There are no hitboxes. After each step the arena reads the normal impulses the solver actually applied, scales them by how fast the two surfaces were closing, and thresholds on speed — which is what separates a footfall from a faceplant, since both carry a large impulse and only one arrives fast. A chimp's overhead smash hurts because 250 N·m of shoulder moved a real arm into a real head.

Getting up

A downed unit aims every limb at the floor beneath its own centre of mass and its torso at the sky. That is the entire get-up controller — one pose, no stages, no clock.

Both details are load-bearing. Aiming in world space rather than in joint angles means "point your shin at the ground" survives being in a tangle, where "flex the hip 1.8 radians" points the leg wherever the pelvis happens to be lying. And aiming inwards rather than straight down gathers the limbs under the load before pressing; straight down makes a splayed body push at its extremities and lever itself onto its head.

Chimpanzees get up from about 90% of knockdowns, humans from about 60% — long legs and a high centre of mass are a real handicap here, which is roughly the honest answer. The get-up strength slider raises the torque ceiling while a unit is down. It is the one assist in here, it is a multiplier on muscle rather than an external force, and at 1× you can watch an unassisted human fail most of the time.
